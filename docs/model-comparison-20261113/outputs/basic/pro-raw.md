# Case 1

In the development cohort, the model achieved an internal-validation AUC of 0.72 (95% CI 0.63 to 0.81), indicating modest discriminative performance in the sample used to build it. On external validation in an independent UK cohort, discrimination fell to 0.64 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.70), a decline that suggests the internal estimate was optimistic and that performance is limited when applied to new patients. Although calibration was assessed, the available evidence does not include decision-curve, workflow-impact, or randomized clinical-utility data. Taken together, these findings show a model with only modest and not fully stable discrimination; they do not yet establish that its use improves decisions or outcomes. Therefore, while the model may have potential for clinical application, any current use should be regarded as exploratory, and it should be evaluated prospectively before being adopted in routine care.

# Case 2

In this observational cohort of 220,089 participants, 8,459 incident breast cancers (3.84%) occurred over a median follow-up of 11.6 years. The poorest sleep score (0-1) was associated with a higher risk than the best score (5) (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.11-1.52), and a metabolic signature showed a stronger association (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.32-1.99). The metabolic signature mediated 13.5% of the observed association between sleep and breast cancer risk, indicating that metabolic factors accounted for a modest share of the relationship. The most important limitation is the observational design, which cannot exclude residual confounding or establish causation; the associations should therefore be interpreted as suggesting, rather than proving, an effect of poor sleep. These findings support the possibility that sleep patterns and related metabolic disturbances are connected to breast cancer risk, but they require confirmation in designs better able to address confounding.

# Case 3

Among 32 consecutive patients undergoing preoperative MRI for endometrial carcinoma staging, overall concordance with final staging was 53.1%. Staging was changed in the remainder: 21.9% were upstaged and 25.0% were downstaged. For myometrial invasion, sensitivity was 58.3%, specificity 65.0%, positive predictive value 50.0%, and negative predictive value 72.2%. These estimates derive from a small, retrospective, single-centre sample, so they are imprecise and may not generalise.

The results should be interpreted with caution and do not support a change in clinical practice on the basis of this study alone.

# Case 5

Material mismatches:

1. Sample size: “32 patients” should be 30 patients.
2. Denominator: “4.77% of contemporaneous admissions” should be 4.77% of contemporaneous AIHA admissions.
3. Sex distribution: “Female patients comprised 66.7%” omits the denominator and the male proportion; the source states 20/30 female (66.7%) and 10/30 male (33.3%).
4. Disease composition omitted: primary disease 26/30 (86.7%) and secondary disease 4/30 (13.3%) are missing.
5. Endpoint claim: “all responses were durable” is not reported; the declining EFS rates contradict an all-durable response claim.
6. Result error: “five-year event-free survival rate was 45.9%” is incorrect; 45.9% is the three-year EFS, and the five-year EFS is 37.5%.
7. Missing results: the one- and three-year EFS rates (56.5% and 45.9%) are not reported.
8. Claim strength: “glucocorticoids cure most patients” exceeds the reported overall response, and “should be the standard treatment for this subtype” is a recommendation not supported by the supplied evidence.

Corrected English version:

This retrospective, single-centre study enrolled 30 patients with DAT-negative AIHA, accounting for 4.77% of contemporaneous AIHA admissions. Of these, 20 were female (66.7%) and 10 were male (33.3%); primary disease was present in 26 patients (86.7%) and secondary disease in 4 patients (13.3%). First-line glucocorticoid therapy achieved an overall response in 93.3% of patients (28/30). After a median follow-up of 64 months, all-cause mortality was 10.0% (3/30). The one-, three-, and five-year event-free survival rates were 56.5%, 45.9%, and 37.5%, respectively.
